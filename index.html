Urban analytics · Paper submitted

Graph-based Neural Network for Informal Settlement Characterization

Luanda · Dhaka · Munich

Project overview

Look at a satellite image of almost any large city in the Global South and you can usually spot the informal settlements. The buildings are smaller, closer together and laid out with less regularity than the neighbourhoods around them. This project asks whether a model can learn that pattern from building footprints alone, without needing imagery at all. On 718,740 labelled footprints from Luanda and Dhaka, graph neural networks beat feature-only baselines in every one of 48 paired runs. They also taught me how easily a small study area, a single split or a badly designed label can mislead.

  • Python
  • PyTorch
  • PyTorch Geometric
  • GeoPandas
  • DuckDB
  • scikit-learn
  • Graph Convolutional Networks
  • Graph Attention Networks

Why graphs?

A single building says very little on its own. What makes a settlement informal is how buildings relate to each other: how close they sit, how they are oriented, and how dense the cluster is. A graph captures that naturally. Each building becomes a node, and neighbouring buildings are joined by edges, so the model learns from both the footprint and its surroundings. Most footprint-based classifiers describe each building by its own geometry and classify it in isolation, which misses exactly this.

Data

Footprints come from the merged Google–Microsoft–OpenStreetMap Open Buildings dataset, queried with DuckDB straight from the remote files so no country-level download is needed. Luanda has 1,167,701 footprints and Dhaka 653,259, and both are used for training and testing. Munich has 186,586 and is held out as an out-of-domain probe: a city with a very different urban form and almost no informal settlement, used to see whether the models learned something general.

I ran the whole experiment twice, first on 1 km squares and then on 10 km squares holding 718,740 labelled footprints.

How it works

Every building gets seven shape descriptors. The graph models and the baselines receive exactly the same inputs, so any difference comes from the graph.

I compare two architectures, Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN) and Graph Attention Networks (GAT). GCNs treat all neighbours equally, while GATs learn which neighbours matter most. I also treat the definition of "neighbour" as an experimental variable, because a graph of buildings is not observed. It has to be constructed, and a neighbourhood of the eight nearest buildings looks very different in a dense settlement than a fixed 50 m radius does.

The labels

No verified informal-settlement boundaries were available for either city, so I built weak labels from four neighbourhood indicators taken from the morphological-slum literature: local density, orientation irregularity, mean neighbour footprint area and spacing irregularity. A building is labelled informal when at least three of the four hold. Every threshold is a percentile within its own city and was fixed before any model was trained. I re-implemented the labelling independently and it reproduced all 1.8 million stored labels exactly.

These labels are a proxy, not ground truth, and every score in this project measures agreement with that proxy.

Results

48 / 48paired runs at 10 km in which a graph model beat the better feature-only baseline
0.65–0.70macro-F1 for the graph models at 10 km, against 0.54–0.57 for the baselines
6.9–10.3 ptsmacro-F1 advantage on the original 1 km areas
0.71–0.73macro-F1 once each city's features are scaled separately

The 10 km experiment used four different held-out edges of each city and two random seeds, which is eight runs per configuration. Graph structure adds real information beyond each building's own shape, under every graph I tested and for both architectures.

What did not hold up

Scaling the study area up by a factor of 100 overturned most of what looked like findings on the small areas.

  • The best graph. At 1 km the distance graph was best for GCN. At 10 km it is narrowly the weakest, though within one standard deviation of the others.
  • GCN versus GAT. They are indistinguishable. Paired on the same split, seed and graph, GAT scores 0.007 lower on average and is ahead in exactly 12 of 24 runs. GCN has fewer parameters, varies less between seeds and trains faster, so I prefer it.
  • Which edge you hold out. The choice of test block moves scores by up to 0.13, more than any modelling choice. All my 1 km results used the hardest one.
  • Munich. At 1 km almost nothing in Munich was predicted informal. At 10 km the rate is 2.1–13.1%, depending on the configuration.

What the labels really encode

Because each threshold is a percentile within one city, the same morphology can be labelled formal in one city and informal in the other. The density threshold, for example, is 38 neighbours in Luanda and 27 in Dhaka. A model trained on Dhaka and tested on Luanda scores below chance (0.40–0.42). Scaling each city's features by its own statistics recovers the performance and makes the results far less sensitive to the split, but it also teaches the model a relative idea of informality that labels a third or more of Munich as informal. Which is right depends on the use. Prioritising areas within one city suits a relative label, while cross-city monitoring, such as for SDG indicator 11.1.1, needs an absolute one.

What the Munich probe showed

The Munich buildings predicted informal are not scattered. They form compact, sharply bounded clusters of very small structures, with a median area of 29 m² against 182 m² for everything else. They look like allotment gardens, which are dense rows of small huts on irregular plots. I have not checked that against land-use data, so it remains a hypothesis. If it holds, the model has learned "many small, closely and irregularly placed structures" and applied it faithfully to fabric that is not housing. That is a real limit of footprint-only methods, and it means a city without informal settlements is not a neutral test for a morphology-based model.

Training at scale

A 10 km area holds up to 462,680 footprints, and the largest graph has 24.9 million edges, which does not fit in memory on my machine. I trained on 1 km tiles with a three-hop halo around each one. For both networks, the predictions for every building in a tile equal those of a full-graph pass, which I proved and then checked numerically (a maximum difference of exactly 0.0). It let me run the whole study on a cloud machine with two cores and no GPU.

Two mistakes I caught

Two errors produced plausible scores while the setup underneath was broken. My first split offset Dhaka's coordinates and then held out the top 25% of the combined range, so the test set was almost entirely Dhaka. It was effectively a train-on-Luanda, test-on-Dhaka experiment, reported as an in-city result, and its macro-F1 of 0.808 was higher than any final result. My first label used compactness, which was also a model input, so a feature-only model could read part of the label directly. Once the split was fixed, the baselines beat the graph models.

Neither error was visible in the score. Both were obvious on a map, so I now plot the predictions before I trust a number.

Limitations

  • The labels are a morphology proxy, and they are built from neighbourhood aggregates that graph models can approximate and feature-only models cannot. Part of the measured gain is therefore a statement that GNNs can recover a neighbourhood-defined label, not independent evidence that they detect true informality better.
  • Only two labelled cities were studied, and the areas are single squares around each city's centre, not deliberately sampled.
  • The orientation feature is measured along an arbitrary side of the bounding rectangle for about 29% of buildings, which adds noise.
  • There is no independent reference data yet, such as community-mapped settlement boundaries.

Status

The work is written up as a paper and submitted to the ISPRS International Journal of Geo-Information. Next I want to validate the labels against independent reference data, choose explicitly between city-relative and absolute labels, check Munich's clusters against land-use data, and extend the tiled training to whole cities and more labelled cities.

Earth observation · In progress

Environmental Indices Analysis Platform

Sentinel-2 · any area of interest

Project overview

I wanted vegetation indices to be easy for anyone to compute, not just for someone who writes code. You give it a boundary, a date range and the indices you want, and the platform returns a time series, a trend, an interactive chart and a map you can download. It answers one question for any polygon that Sentinel-2 covers: how has vegetation, or surface water, behaved here over this period? No code is required on your end.

  • Python
  • FastAPI
  • Dask
  • STAC
  • xarray
  • Streamlit
  • Docker

What it does

The platform computes NDVI, EVI and SAVI for vegetation, and MNDWI for surface water, from Sentinel-2 Level-2A imagery found through a STAC catalogue (Microsoft Planetary Computer by default). The area can be a bounding box or an uploaded GeoJSON file. For each index it returns the mean, minimum, maximum and standard deviation of the time series, a linear trend in index units per year, the time series itself with the number of clear days and the clear-sky share of the area for each period, and a time-mean composite map. The map comes as a georeferenced GeoTIFF for GIS software, a titled PNG figure for reports and a transparent overlay for web maps.

How it works

A Streamlit front end sends each request to a FastAPI service. Large areas can take minutes, so the service returns a job identifier straight away and does the work in the background while the interface polls for the result. Dask does the heavy lifting. Everything between the search and the final statistics is a lazy graph, and pixel data is only read when all the results are computed together in a single step.

The order of these steps is deliberate, because several are only correct in this sequence. Masks must come before merging, because the merge keeps the first clear value at each pixel across a day's tiles, and a cloudy pixel that had not been masked could win over a clear one from the overlapping tile. Nodata must be masked before the reflectance offset is added, or an empty pixel would become a reflectance of −0.1 and enter the averages.

What I learned along the way

Every defect I found was in the data processing, not in the web layer. I found them by running the system against real Sentinel-2 data and by writing tests for each one.

  • Unscaled reflectance. Sentinel-2 stores reflectance as integers up to 10,000, and EVI needs values between 0 and 1. With the raw numbers, EVI came out in the hundreds of thousands. Scaling each collection before any index maths fixed it.
  • A hidden offset. Since early 2022 ESA adds 1,000 to the stored values, and a single search can return scenes with different processing baselines. Ignoring the offset inflates every ratio index's denominator. Over a dense forest near Nairobi, mean NDVI rose from 0.33 to 0.55 once I applied it per scene.
  • Reading everything. The engine fetched every band for every index, even when an index needs only two. Fetching only what each index uses halved the run time of a test run, from 121 seconds to 60.5, with identical NDVI.
  • Bounding box, not boundary. For Nairobi County, 36.5% of the bounding box lies outside the boundary, so I added a polygon mask. It is built lazily in blocks, which shrank it inside the task graph from 15.3 MiB to 9.6 KiB.
  • Half a pixel. The library labels pixels by their corners and the GeoTIFF writer assumes centres, which shifted exported maps by 5 m. Every step now uses pixel centres, and a test checks the file's origin.
  • Partial coverage. A county spans several satellite tiles, and each tile only covers part of it. Before merging same-day tiles, clear-sky coverage looked like 38–50% and the January to March NDVI looked like 0.367. After merging, coverage was 75–98% and NDVI was 0.262.

Results so far

996 km²Nairobi County, January to March 2023: 42 scenes in about 7 minutes on a home connection
17 testsin about 5 seconds, no network needed, aimed at the numerical core
2×faster once each index reads only its own bands
Karuraforest test area: NDVI of 0.551 after the reflectance offset fix

Run time is dominated by download volume. It scales with the area, the number of scenes and the number of bands read, so a year over the same county should take several times longer than three months.

Data handling

Imagery is never stored. Workers read only the pixel windows covering the area, straight from cloud-optimised GeoTIFFs, and discard them after the computation. Inputs are not persisted, results expire after 24 hours, and signed URLs are removed from any error sent back to the client. The polygon is sent to the STAC catalogue as the search geometry, so the catalogue operator can see the area searched.

Known limitations

  • EVI is an approximation for now. The band mapping does not yet register Sentinel-2's blue band, so green stands in for it. NDVI, SAVI and MNDWI are not affected. Adding the band is a one-line change.
  • Projection. Data is resampled to Web Mercator at a nominal 10 m. Ground resolution and area drift away from the equator, so a native UTM grid would be more accurate.
  • Period means. Each clear day counts equally, however little of the area it observed. Weighting by the valid fraction is a possible refinement.
  • Landsat. It is registered but untested and has no per-pixel cloud masking.
  • Not ready for public use. There is no authentication yet, and the job store lives in one process. These must be fixed before a public deployment.

Status

The full stack runs end to end in Docker, with the API and interface as separate services. The Dask cluster can run locally or on AWS Fargate, but I have only tried the local mode on real data. Next I want to run the whole interface against live Sentinel-2 data on a machine with a fast connection, add the blue band, and then deploy to AWS.

Mobility Analysis from Call Detail Records

Call detail records · 1.5M users

Project overview

During the COVID-19 lockdowns in 2020, governments used mobile network data to check whether movement restrictions were holding. Call Detail Records (CDRs) make that possible: every connection to a cell tower leaves a trace that estimates where a user is over time. This project builds the processing side of that idea, a PySpark pipeline that turns raw CDRs for 1.5 million users into per-user mobility metrics.

  • Python
  • PySpark
  • Pandas UDFs
  • NumPy
  • Seaborn
  • Matplotlib

Objectives

Process call records for 1.5 million users with PySpark, generate summary statistics, and explore the relationship between the average number of unique locations per user per day and the average radius of gyration.

Data

Call records for 1.5 million users over 14 days, from 29 June to 13 July 2018. Each record is joined to cell-tower coordinates, and records missing a cell ID, latitude or longitude are dropped.

Methods and workflow

Preprocessing runs in Spark. The radius of gyration, the distance a user typically moves from their centre of activity, is computed with a pandas UDF, which is much faster than converting the Spark DataFrame to pandas first. Repartitioning, caching and sampling during debugging keep the pipeline scalable.

Findings

Activity per user is heavily skewed: the median user has 3 records, the mean is about 7.8, three quarters of users have 8 or fewer, and the most active user has 8,277. Average radius of gyration and average unique locations per day are positively but modestly correlated (r = 0.32).

A Geospatial Language Model

Qwen2.5-Coder 1.5B · runs on a laptop

Project overview

Ask a code model to find the buildings within 500 m of a road and it will often hand you code that runs, looks confident and is wrong. If the layer is in degrees, a 500 m buffer is a 500 degree buffer. Nothing crashes and nothing warns you. This project asks how far a small model, trained on my own laptop, can be taught to avoid that kind of mistake, and how to measure the result honestly.

  • Python
  • LoRA
  • MLX
  • Qwen2.5-Coder
  • GeoPandas
  • pyproj
  • Shapely

Why a small model

Three things made this a good fit. I can check the output, because code either runs or it does not, and I can compare the result with a known answer. The job is narrow, so a model with 1.5 billion parameters can get good at a few tasks without having to be a general assistant. And the data is cheap, because answers can be verified by running them, so I can generate training examples and throw away any that fail.

Three routes

I planned three routes and I am working through them in order. First, fine-tune a small open model with LoRA, the fastest way to a result I can measure. Nine versions are trained so far. Second, add tools and checks around the model, so it does not have to remember what a program can look up. Three pieces are built. Third, train a small transformer from scratch, to see what pre-training really contributes. That one has not started.

So far the model covers four GeoPandas tasks: selecting features within a distance, counting points per polygon, measuring polygon area, and filtering by attribute.

How it is trained and tested

The whole project rests on one idea. Generate examples whose answers can be checked by running them, train on those, and test on map layers the model has never seen.

Each prompt carries its own schema, with layer names, columns and coordinate system, so the model has to read them instead of recalling them. Training and test layers come from separate name pools. Every schema exists in several coordinate systems, so "this layer is metric" cannot stand in for actually reading the CRS. Scoring forgives harmless differences, such as the right buildings returned in a different CRS.

One mistake of mine is worth telling. My first counting template passed every check while quietly dropping zones that had zero features. Execution proved the code ran, not that it was right. I only found it by reading the reference answers line by line.

Where I was wrong

Most of what I learned came from explanations I believed and then had to give up.

  • The baseline. The untuned model scored 0 of 42, which I nearly recorded as a result about the model. It was my prompt. I never named the language, so it answered in SQL. With the prompt fixed it scored 15 of 42.
  • A confident wrong sentence. After the first fine-tune, five of the seven misses came from one schema, where the model wrote that the layers were "already in a metric CRS (EPSG:4326)". That is false. In my data the coordinate system was tied to the schema, so the model copied the sentence without reading the code.
  • Three codes were not enough. On codes it had never seen, the model failed on every geographic code except 4326. It had learned "reproject only for 4326", and my own template, which tested for 4326 directly, taught it that.
  • A score that could not fail. For some distance prompts the correct answer was every feature in the layer, so a model that ignored the CRS still scored as correct. Of one version's 31 correct answers on unseen geographic codes, 29 were of this kind. I rebuilt the test.
  • The mystery of "cafes". One version failed 18 times, every time on a single layer name. I blamed a lack of name variety, which was wrong. I still do not know the full cause.

The fix: let a tool tell the model

A 1.5 billion parameter model has no reliable way to know which of thousands of EPSG codes use degrees, and it does not need one. A program can look that up. So a small function asks pyproj for each layer's coordinate system type and unit, and writes the answer straight into the prompt:

facilities(fid int, geometry Point EPSG:4269 [geographic, unit: degree])

Now the model reads a fact instead of recalling one. The comparison that mattered used fresh geographic codes I had not looked at while tuning. Version 6, which writes its reasoning before the code, reprojected on 85 of 144 of those prompts. Version 7, with the annotation, reprojected on all of them.

I then showed it the wrong label on purpose. In all 192 prompts its decision followed the label and ignored the code number. That is what I wanted from a trusted tool. It also means the tool has to be right.

The system around the model

The scores only hold if the model receives prompts in the exact format it was trained on. A chat box wired straight to it would perform far worse. So the real system wraps the model in ordinary code that I can test.

  • CRS lookup tool. Reads each layer's coordinate system and writes it into the prompt. If there is none, it says so instead of guessing.
  • Name checker. Parses the model's code without running it and checks every name against the schema. It caught all 33 name and syntax errors in my saved predictions and flagged none of 2,557 correct answers.
  • Careful auto-repair. Fixes an obvious typo in a layer name. If two fixes are equally likely, it does nothing, because a silent wrong guess is worse than a crash.

Teaching it to say "I don't know"

Real layers often have no coordinate system at all, like a shapefile that lost its .prj file. Before version 8 the model simply invented one. It claimed EPSG:4326 and reprojected, in all 90 of 90 test cases. Now, when a distance or an area is needed, it asks which CRS to use. For counting and attribute filters it gets on with the work, because the answer does not depend on units.

Asking is only useful if the next turn works. My first version asked correctly and then ignored the answer, repeating the question every time (0 of 96 follow-ups). Only a two-turn test showed me that. Version 9 sets the CRS the user names and carries on, and gets 94 of 96 right.

Results so far

970 / 970on the held-out templated test, with no needless questions asked
94 / 96on two-turn follow-ups. Both misses are a drifted variable name
33 / 33name and syntax errors caught by the checker
0 / 2,557correct answers wrongly flagged

Please read these with care. Every test uses templated wording, and many prompts differ only in the coordinate system or one number, so they tend to pass or fail together. The scores show that the model learned this task for these prompts. How it will do with real people's wording, I do not know yet.

What it cannot do yet

  • Only four operations are covered. There is no dissolve, nearest neighbour, overlay or raster work.
  • It does not handle layers with a mix of known and unknown coordinate systems, free answers such as "I think it is UTM 37", or projected systems in feet.
  • It always reprojects to UTM zone 37S. That suits Nairobi and gets worse the further you go. The fix will follow the same idea as the CRS tool: let a program choose the metric CRS and show it in the prompt.

Status

Work in progress. Next is a small local demo with a guided task picker, so I can see how the model copes with real wording and real data. Requests from the demo will seed a proper paraphrase test. After that I plan a harder test set, PostGIS SQL, STAC queries, a retry loop, and the from-scratch transformer.

LLM Workflow · In use

An LLM-Assisted Application Workflow

Claude · custom skills · LaTeX · Python

Project overview

Applying for PhD positions, fellowships and jobs means writing the same story many times: a CV, a letter and answers to portal questions, each cut to a different advert and page limit. A language model makes the drafting fast. It also makes three mistakes that matter more in an application than almost anywhere else. It drifts from the facts, it quietly drops things to fit a page, and it writes in a voice that is not yours. I built a small system around Claude to keep the speed and catch those mistakes, and I have used it on five real applications.

  • Claude
  • Custom skills
  • Python
  • LaTeX
  • YAML
  • Shell

The problem I was solving

  • Drift. A model rounds a figure, merges two roles or describes a method I never used. Each slip is small, and each one is hard to spot when I am rereading my own work.
  • Quiet cuts. To fit a page limit, it removes an award or a thesis title and does not mention it.
  • A generic voice. The drafts read like a list of achievements instead of an argument made by a person.

My answer was to treat application writing as a small piece of software. Facts live in one verified place. Claude drafts by following written procedures. A script builds every document and another script checks it before I see it. I stay in charge of everything that cannot be undone.

How it fits together

There are five layers. Information moves down from verified facts to finished documents, and two loops move back up: failed checks return to drafting, and facts I confirm return to the knowledge layer.

  • Knowledge. A folder with my profile split by topic, a machine-readable facts.yaml, the source documents (thesis, transcripts, posters), approved answers to recurring questions, and samples of my own writing. Uncertain items are marked [TO CONFIRM]. It also records what I have not done, so a letter can name a gap instead of implying skill I do not have.
  • Agent. Claude, working through five skills: one that orchestrates an application, and ones for CVs, cover letters, portal questions and quality review. A skill is a written procedure, and it holds no facts about me. Every fact is read from the profile.
  • Build and check. Scripts that compile the LaTeX, enforce page limits, check the facts and render each page as an image to look at.
  • Output. One folder per application, with its requirements, a status log, the sources and the PDFs.

Life of one application

The first stage is a recommendation, not a decision. For one job advert, the analysis showed that the role's core requirement, advanced Stata, was missing from my profile. I decided to pass, and no folder was created. That felt like the system working as intended.

Each folder keeps a requirements.md (parts, limits, deadline with its time zone) and a status.md (stage, open items, a dated log). If I come back after a week, I can see at a glance what is done and what is left.

Checking the facts

The checker, check_facts.py, compares every document against facts.yaml and exits with an error if anything fails, so it can gate a build.

  • Forbidden wording. Patterns that must never appear, each with the reason. A superseded figure, a wrong tool name and a method my thesis did not use are all on this list.
  • Identity. Any ORCID-shaped string, email address or phone number must equal the one on file.
  • Dates near titles. A different date range next to a role is a failure. No date at all is only a warning, because titles also appear in prose.
  • CV completeness. Things every CV must contain, such as both thesis titles and every award.
  • Style. Phrases I want to avoid in letters, such as "passionate", are flagged for rewording.

The forbidden list grows from real mistakes. When a draft contained a wrong figure or a misspelt organisation, I fixed it and then added the wording to the list so it could not return.

Looking at the pages

A PDF can compile cleanly and still be wrong. A line runs past the margin, a stray character prints as text, or a page break strands a signature. So looking at the pages is a required step. The build script renders every page to an image, Claude opens each one, and the text is also extracted to confirm that ligatures and symbols survive copying and applicant-tracking systems. If an image cannot be viewed, Claude is told to say so rather than report a check that did not happen.

Getting the voice right

My early drafts were correct and dull. They had bold sub-headings, bullet points inside letters, and paragraphs that strung facts together ("At X, I did Y. I also did Z."). I gave Claude paragraphs from letters I had written before, and wrote down what they share: open with a problem in the field, then show where my interest comes from; build the foundation as connected work in service of an idea; then look forward to what I would investigate. Gaps are stated in one or two plain sentences, and numbers appear only where they make a point, because the CV carries the full figures. The difference shows most in the first sentence. A list-style draft opened with "I am applying for the fully funded PhD position in your lab". The revised one opens with a statement about the problem and reaches the position once it has been set up.

What the checks caught

These are problems that really reached a draft. Each one became a permanent rule.

  • A superseded figure from my thesis, caught by comparing with the thesis. It is now a forbidden pattern.
  • A claim that a model used rainfall and temperature when the thesis used rainfall only.
  • Misspelt names of tools and organisations, caught by review against sources.
  • Tailoring that removed thesis details and awards from CVs. I noticed it myself, and the "kept in full" rule now lives in the CV skill.
  • A stray carriage return that printed as garbage in a CV. Only the page image showed it, which is why visual review is mandatory.
  • A --date option that did nothing. The compiler was silently reading the original file instead of the modified copy. I only saw it by looking at the rendered date. The fix was to put the temporary folder first on the search path.
  • A signature that pushed a statement onto a second page. The page-limit exit code now stops the build.

Where I draw the line

  • I submit every application, send every email and sign in to every portal. The system never stores or enters a password.
  • New facts enter my profile only after I confirm them. If something disagrees, it is flagged, not silently corrected.
  • Gaps are stated honestly. A modelled estimate is never presented as an observed result, a poster is never presented as a publication, and a missing document is never replaced by a different one.
  • Supervisor emails are drafted only when I ask for them.

These rules are written into the skills, so they hold when a session is resumed later or on another device.

What it cannot do yet

  • The fact checker matches patterns. It catches known wrong wording and missing items, but not a new error phrased in a new way. That still depends on the quality review and on me.
  • Visual review depends on the model inspecting page images. It is thorough for layout and is no substitute for me reading the final PDF.
  • The date rule produces warnings in prose, where dates are rarely near titles.
  • Builds on my Mac lack the icon package that the letter template wants, so letters there use text icons unless I rebuild them with my own TeX installation.
  • Deadlines live in the status files and in reminders I set myself, not in the system.

Status

In use on five applications: three PhD positions and two jobs. It also supports interview preparation, with each story written once in situation, task, action and result form and checked against the same facts. Next I want unit tests for the checker, one command that runs every build, check and package step before submission, a question bank for interviews, and a public repository with the scripts, templates and skills, with my personal data removed.
